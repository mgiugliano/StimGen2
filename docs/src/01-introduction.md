# Introduction {#sec:intro}

In a typical intracellular experiment, a neuron receives a sequence of
current or voltage commands: a family of steps to measure its input
resistance and firing rate, a ramp to find its rheobase, a chirp to measure
its impedance, a noisy current to probe the reliability of its spike times.
Each of these commands is, in the end, a list of numbers sent to a D/A
converter at a fixed rate. Yet nobody thinks of a stimulus as a list of
numbers. We think of it as "half a second at rest, then one second at
300 pA, then back to rest".

StimGen 2 is a small language that lets us write stimuli exactly as we think
of them. The step above is the text

```
500ms  dc(0)
1s     dc(300)
500ms  dc(0)
```

and a five-second sinusoid riding on a noisy background is

```
5s  sine(amp=50, freq=8Hz) + ou(mean=0, sd=20, tau=5ms)
```

These texts are converted into samples only when the stimulus is played.

## Why describe stimuli as text

Writing stimuli as short symbolic texts, rather than storing their samples,
has several advantages, and they are the design goals of StimGen 2.

- **Compactness.** The length of a description does not grow with the
  duration of the stimulus. Ten minutes of noise take one line.
- **Independence of the hardware.** A description does not depend on the
  sampling rate or on the resolution of the converter. The same file can be
  played at 10 kHz on one set-up and at 50 kHz on another.
- **Readability and editability.** A stimulus can be read, checked, and
  modified in any text editor, generated by a script, or typed directly on
  the command line.
- **Precision.** Every description has one exact meaning, defined
  mathematically in Part II, so that two implementations produce the same
  samples.
- **Reproducibility.** Stochastic stimuli are reproducible from their
  description and a seed, and the seed is always recorded. Every stimulus
  that was ever played can be regenerated, years later, from the record
  stored with the data.

## Three layers

StimGen 2 distinguishes three layers, each built on the previous one.

1. A **waveform** describes what one output does during one trial. It is a
   sequence of *segments*, each lasting a given time and built from
   elementary *generators* such as constants, ramps, sinusoids, and noise
   processes. Generators can be added, multiplied, and transformed.
2. A **stimulus** combines several waveforms on named output channels, with
   their units and a common time base, and adds *markers* that label
   instants of interest in the recorded data.
3. A **protocol** describes a whole set of trials: which parameter is
   varied and over which values, how many times each condition is
   repeated, in which order, and how much time separates the trials.

Most users will spend most of their time with waveforms. Stimuli and
protocols are needed as soon as an experiment involves more than one output
or more than one trial, which is to say very soon.

All three are written in plain text files with the extension `.sg`.

## How to read this document

Part I, which you are reading, is a tutorial. It introduces the language
through examples and assumes no more than familiarity with
electrophysiology. Part II is the reference specification: it defines every
construct precisely, using a little mathematics, and is written for
implementers and for users who want to know exactly what a description
does. The tutorial refers to the relevant sections of Part II as it goes.
Appendix A is a glossary, and Appendix B contains the derivations of the
formulas.

Every figure in this document was produced by the reference renderer `sg`
from the StimGen 2 text printed next to it, and plotted with the small
reader `tools/sgplot.py` (`docs/figures/make_figures.py` regenerates them
all).
